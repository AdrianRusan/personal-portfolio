DX measured AI coding lifting PR throughput by just under 8% in a sample drawn from 400 companies. On my own backlog I estimate 2–3x. The part I can measure: my agent PRs merged a median 44 minutes after they opened, review and fixes included, while LinearB's AI PRs waited over 16 hours on average just for a reviewer to pick them up.

**Short answer:** For one senior engineer reviewing a scoped backlog of agent-written PRs, 2–3x faster is my estimate. The measured part: in March 2026, 30 agent PRs on my own dashboard merged a median 44 minutes after they opened, in 9 review sittings over under three days. In LinearB's 2026 data, AI-written PRs wait more than 16 hours just for a reviewer to pick them up. My explanation, which the timestamps support and can't prove: the review queue is one person who wrote the tickets.

## What 2026 measured across teams

The large studies find small gains, and I take them seriously because they measure delivery, not typing:

- **DX, 11 Mar 2026:** in a random sample drawn from 400 companies (Nov 2024 to Feb 2026), AI usage rose 65% and PR throughput rose 7.76%. Most organizations landed between 5% and 15%. ([DX newsletter](https://newsletter.getdx.com/p/ai-productivity-gains-are-10-not)) DX's benchmarks updated on 21 Jul 2026 put year-on-year PR throughput up nearly 20%. ([DX benchmarks](https://getdx.com/blog/2026-dx-benchmarks-are-now-available/))
- **LinearB, 4 May 2026:** across 8.1 million PRs from 4,800 teams, AI-generated PRs waited more than 16 hours on average before a reviewer picked them up, against about 200 minutes for unassisted work. Only 32.7% of AI PRs merged within 30 days, against 84.5% of manual ones. ([LinearB](https://linearb.io/blog/8-million-prs-engineering-productivity))
- **Faros, 23 Jul 2025:** on teams with high AI adoption, PRs merged rose 98% and PR review time rose 91%, with no company-level improvement in throughput or DORA metrics. ([Faros AI](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-software-engineering))
- **METR, 24 Feb 2026:** its 2025 trial had experienced developers 19% slower with AI. The 2026 update estimates 18% faster for the original group and 4% faster for new recruits, with confidence intervals that cross zero, and METR calls it "only very weak evidence". ([METR](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/))

LinearB and Faros put the delay at review, and DX and METR find gains that are small or uncertain. DX's July figure of nearly 20% is year-on-year throughput from all causes, not a measured AI effect. Against that data, 2–3x is the high claim, so it needs its arithmetic shown.

## My clock: 30 agent PRs, from open to merge

In March 2026 an agent fleet opened 31 PRs on my own admin dashboard and I merged 30 after review; the full account, including what review caught and missed, is in [the 30-PR case study](/work/30-reviewed-prs-3-vulnerabilities). Here is only the clock, pulled from GitHub with `gh pr list --json createdAt,mergedAt` on 12 Oct 2026:

| Measure | Value | How it was obtained |
|---|---|---|
| PRs merged / opened | 30 / 31 | GitHub |
| Open to merge, median | 44 min | GitHub timestamps |
| Open to merge, middle half | 28–62 min | GitHub timestamps |
| Open to merge, 90th percentile | 2 h | GitHub timestamps |
| Slowest two | 9.5 h and 17.6 h | GitHub timestamps |
| Review sittings (merges less than 30 min apart) | 9 | GitHub timestamps |
| First PR opened to last merge | 2 days 19 hours | GitHub timestamps |
| Merged PRs with a fix commit from review | 25 of 30 | git history |
| My active hours | unknown | the transcripts were deleted after Claude Code's 30-day default |
| The same work done solo | 5–7 working days | **my estimate** |

The October run on this site has the same shape: [nine PRs](/work/nine-prs-one-afternoon) merged a median 27 minutes after opening, in 2 sittings. Across all 36 PRs merged on this repo from 10 to 12 October, the median was 27 minutes and the 90th percentile 2.3 hours.

Here is the division. The solo side is my estimate of 5–7 working days. The elapsed side is 2.1 days from the first worker wave to the last merge, or 2.8 days from the first PR, nights included. 5–7 days over 2.1–2.8 days gives 1.8x to 3.3x, which I round to 2–3x and quote as an estimate. Of 30 PRs, one waited longer than 16 hours. It was #95, the first PR, opened a day before the worker waves started; the second slowest, #97 at 9.5 hours, redid a PR I had closed.

## Three conditions I think explain the gap

Three things differ from the teams in those studies. Each one is a condition, so check whether your setup has it.

1. **The queue is one person who is expecting the PRs.** I dispatched each agent wave and then sat down to review it. Merges landed in 9 bursts, and PR size didn't predict the wait in March: the rank correlation between lines changed and minutes waited was 0.07. The wait was set by when I sat down. My guess is that LinearB's 16 hours is mostly reviewers who weren't expecting the PR.
2. **The reviewer wrote the tickets.** I scoped every issue in that run, so I knew what each diff was supposed to do before I opened it. That shortens the reading, and it is something a team reviewer picking up a stranger's agent PR doesn't have.
3. **Almost everything merges.** 30 of 31 PRs merged, and 25 of them needed a fix commit first. Rejected work is the most expensive kind, and in LinearB's data two thirds of AI PRs hadn't merged after 30 days.

Where this breaks:

- **A team of ten.** Review becomes a queue between people again, and the 16 hours comes back unless someone owns pickup time.
- **Unclear or greenfield work.** My runs were backlog and feature work against an existing system. I haven't measured a vague "build me a product" brief, and I expect it to go slower than solo while the rewriting happens.
- **Bigger PRs as the habit.** My median PR changed 388 lines, close to LinearB's 400+ at the 75th percentile for AI PRs. In October, across 36 PRs, bigger ones did wait longer (rank correlation 0.56).
- **The ramp.** It took months of harness work (rules, hooks, review gates) before runs like these held up. Expect your first weeks to be slower than doing it yourself.

## What review cost, and what it still missed

Review is counted against the clock, and it isn't free. 25 of 30 PRs needed a fix commit before merge. Review also missed things: it fixed 3 shell-injection paths from request input before merge, and missed 5 security bugs, found when I re-audited the run on 12 October 2026: 3 shell strings, one of them from request input (#98), and 2 stored XSS sinks. All 5 are still open in that repo. [The case study](/work/30-reviewed-prs-3-vulnerabilities#what-review-missed) has the list.

Speed has a price here too: #109, one of the 5 misses, merged 29 minutes after it opened, in a sitting that merged two PRs in 13 seconds. It's one data point, and it is a miss that's still open.

That is why I won't quote a bigger number. A faster figure is available if you drop the review, and the misses above happened with it.

## Measure your own review wait this week

You can get the measured half of this for your team from data GitHub already keeps:

```bash
gh pr list --state merged --limit 200 --json number,author,createdAt,mergedAt,additions,deletions \
  | jq -r '.[] | [.number, .author.login, ((((.mergedAt|fromdate) - (.createdAt|fromdate)) / 60) | floor)] | @tsv'
```

If your agents open PRs under a human's login, as mine do, split them by branch prefix or label instead of author. Then track three numbers, agent PRs against the rest:

1. **Open-to-merge time, median and 90th percentile.** If agent PRs wait hours, the speed-up is going into the queue.
2. **Fix commits per PR before merge.** This is the review cost. Mine was 25 of 30 PRs with at least one.
3. **Review sittings.** Merges that land minutes apart are one sitting. In my runs, a few planned sittings kept the median wait under an hour.

If the median wait for agent PRs is in hours, the cheapest gain is a named owner for pickup time, before any new tool.
